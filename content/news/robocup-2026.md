RoboCup has a way of making abstract robotics problems very concrete. From 30 June to 6 July, we were in Incheon, South Korea, for RoboCup 2026, rUNSWift’s first world competition with the Booster K1 humanoid robots.

Moving from the familiar NAO platform to the K1 was an ambitious change. In a short preparation period, the team had to adapt locomotion, localisation, game play and our ROS 2 software to a very different robot. Unsurprisingly, competition exposed things that were difficult to see in the lab. That is part of why we go: real matches reveal very quickly which parts of a system are robust and which still need work.

For me, the most valuable part was seeing the students work through those problems together. They diagnosed failures on the field, made decisions under genuine time pressure and learned how software, hardware and teamwork interact when there is no extension to the deadline. We came home with a much clearer idea of what to improve next, as well as the experience of getting a new generation of robots onto the field.
